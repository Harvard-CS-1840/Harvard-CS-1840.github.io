Introduction

Modern AI systems often need the ability to make sequential decisions in an unknown, uncertain, possibly hostile environment, by actively interacting with the environment to collect relevant data. Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a general framework that can capture the interactive learning setting and has been used to design intelligent agents that achieve high-level performance in challenging applications such as Go, computer games, robotic manipulation, health care, education, and the training of large language models.

This course provides an introduction to reinforcement learning covering a range of problem formulations, algorithms, and theory. The five main themes of the course are:

  1. tabular planning (the Markov decision process formalism, Bellman equations, policy evaluation, value iteration, policy iteration),
  2. exploration (multi-armed bandits, regret, explore-then-commit, epsilon-greedy, upper confidence bounds, contextual bandits),
  3. value-based learning (temporal-difference learning, Q-learning, tabular model-based RL and the simulation lemma, function approximation, deep Q networks),
  4. imitation learning (behavior cloning, DAgger), and
  5. policy optimization (REINFORCE, variance reduction, natural policy gradient, PPO, learning from human feedback and from verifiable rewards).

There will also be an Embedded Ethics lecture on ethical issues arising in reinforcement learning.

The assignments will focus on a mix of algorithmic and statistical principles, along with their programming implementations. After taking this course, students will be able to state, implement, and analyze the fundamental RL algorithms.

Homework

Homework materials: Homework assignments can be downloaded from Canvas. There are five homeworks, released every two weeks; You will have an additional week for HW3 to help prepare for the midterm and an additional week for HW5 since it is a more open-ended assignment. Each homework contains a math portion and a programming portion.

  1. For the math portion, you must submit a single PDF. You may choose to neatly handwrite your work or typeset it using a markup language such as LaTeX or Typst. See overleaf.com/learn or typst.app/docs/tutorial for the respective tutorials.
  2. For the programming portion, you will submit a zip archive of your codebase in the same file structure as the downloaded assignment. This includes your README.md file with any written responses. See markdownguide.org/getting-started for a guide to Markdown formatting. You do not need to submit a PDF for this portion. If you typeset the math portion, you must include the source code in your zip archive for verification purposes.

Compute requirements: The programming assignments are designed to be runnable on a personal laptop. We will also provide access to cloud CPUs through HUIT. Further information and setup instructions will be posted on Ed.

Homework submission: Homeworks will be submitted through Gradescope. Pages must be correctly assigned to questions in order to be graded.

Late homeworks: Each student will have 96 cumulative hours of late time (as measured on Gradescope). Up to 48 hours may be used on any one assignment. Any assignment that is turned in beyond this limit will receive zero credit.

Regrade requests: All regrade requests must be submitted on Gradescope within seven days after the grades for the homework in question are released. This ensures that we can address concerns in a timely and fair manner. Grades will only be changed in the case of a clear mis-application of the rubric.

Homework grading: HW0 is graded on completion (effort shown, not correctness). For the remaining homeworks 1–5, each question is worth some indicated number of points, and your final homework score will be a weighted average across all homework questions with respect to these points. In order to pass the course, you must attempt and submit all homeworks, even for zero credit.

Collaboration: Collaboration is encouraged under the restriction that you fully own your submissions and cite any help you received. This means that when you submit a homework, you affirm that:

  • The entirety of your submission was written by you. Including output from a peer or an AI tool into your submission is strictly prohibited.
  • Your submission honestly represents your own understanding of the material. If a teaching staff member asks you about any part of your submission and finds that it is not your own work, it will be treated as academic misconduct.
  • You have not shared, and will not share, any part of your submission as-is with a classmate or outside of the class. For example, you may not look at a peer's submission or post your solution publicly on Ed. (Discussing your thought process or asking questions is welcome and encouraged.)

In each of your answers, you must list all your collaborators (human or AI), as well as any materials that you consulted (except those on the course website). To support your recall, we recommend you wait a day between receiving help and writing up your own answer.

Honor code and AI policy

You are held to the Harvard College Honor Code. See the section above on collaboration. To further elaborate:

  • Do not use forums like Course Hero, Chegg, etc.
  • Do not directly ask or search for answers, either from a peer, on the Internet, or from an AI tool.
  • Properly cite any outside materials you use for your homeworks. If you are unclear about whether some online material can be used, please ask the course staff first.
  • Do not share of your solutions within or outside class at any time.
  • The same principles apply to generative AI tools as human collaborators: you can use them for assistance, and if you do, you should cite them, but you may not directly ask them for the answer.

The above is not an exhaustive list, and in general, common sense rules about academic integrity apply. If something is in doubt, please ask us for permission before you do it.

Textbooks: There are many high-quality open resources available that intersect with our course material. This reading is strictly supplementary: course assessments will be based solely on material covered in lectures and homeworks.

  • S&B: Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction by Richard Sutton and Andrew Barto. This is the classic text in reinforcement learning.
  • ABJKS: Reinforcement Learning: Theory and Algorithms by Alekh Agarwal, Kianté Brantley, Nan Jiang, Sham Kakade, and Wen Sun. This is an advanced RL theory book that is mostly out of the scope of this class. Feedback is encouraged and should be sent to bookrltheory [at] gmail [dot] com.
